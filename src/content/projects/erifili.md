## The problem

In the past year, about one percent of young Israelis around the age of 30 emigrated. For them,
physical distance makes it hard to keep their **tribe**. Friendships move into screens, and the
effort to bridge different life rhythms and time zones runs in the background. The people who were
once the closest slowly drift apart.

Social networks offer endless ways to share (text, video, voice), but human conversation doesn't
fully survive the move. Closeness is synchronised technically, not emotionally, and the public
nature of most platforms makes sharing superficial.

## Where it comes from

Erifili was born from a personal need. My childhood friends and I used to meet after school, sit
in the square and eat toast. Over the years the toast became beer, and we moved to the city
together. Then, at twenty, we set off to travel the world, from South America through Asia to
Australia. We thought it was a short chapter. It wasn't: we scattered around the world, and now we
live in different time zones and rarely meet.

This isn't only my story. War, political and economic change, a pandemic and climate are moving
people everywhere, and teenagers whose families move abroad lose their tribe at an especially
vulnerable age. That's why Erifili is aimed at **teenagers and young adults**, as prevention
rather than cure.

## Research

- **The science of friendship.** A ten-year study of 169 teenagers found that close friendships, not
  popularity, predict better mental health in adulthood (Narr et al., _Child Development_, 2017).
- **Interviews** with older adults, some of whom had moved between countries, showed the same
  pattern: memories of old friends bring a smile, but over the years the bonds loosened, more so
  among those who emigrated.
- **A worldwide questionnaire** based on the McGill Friendship Questionnaire. Of those who answered,
  72.8% said they and their childhood friends rarely meet, 67.5% said shared memories still affect
  their day, 63% felt they had grown apart, and only 21% keep in touch through social networks.
- **Teenagers** prefer text and visual chat over voice calls and live with high self-awareness and
  fear of judgment. They are the audience the app is designed for.

## Process

The project began with the body. It moved from wearables and gloves, through **touch without
touch** (how can you feel close to someone who isn't here?), to hands-on experiments with sensor
kits: pulse, moisture, EEG, textiles and scents.

Mapping how we communicate (intentional or not, direct or indirect, synchronous or asynchronous)
led to the idea of a shared, asynchronous journal, a memory capsule for friends. That became
Erifili. Along the way I coded prototypes for **face filters** that shape a photo into a
character, **dual presence** (two webcams that bring friends in two places into one scene) and
**bio-feedback** from breathing and facial expressions.

## The app

**Erifili** is an intimate social network for you and your closest friends, built around a
**shared journal** for ongoing communication and memories.

- **An avatar as your digital twin.** It starts from your photo and is designed with AI tools and
  your own choices. Once a week, three questions refresh it: tell your friends something exciting
  that happened this week, something disappointing, and something you're looking forward to.
- **A world that feels.** Each friend lives in their own city, built from the photos and moments
  they share, and the cities are stacked like a totem, with friends who are asleep at the base. The
  colours follow each person's time of day (pastels in the morning, neon at night), season,
  weather and place.
- **Calm by design.** No screenshots, no timestamps beyond the date, no pressure to share: at most
  three posts a day, so the app never feels like surveillance or an obligation.
- **Conversation textures (planned).** The tone of the conversation will be analysed with AI and
  will shape how the personal and group spaces look.

_The interface is not meant to replace the physical world._

## Why avatars

Teenagers and young adults live with heightened self-awareness. An avatar is a gentle mask: _“I am
my filter.”_ I can send a picture of myself crying and feel more comfortable, because it isn't
quite me. The avatars convey more complex empathy than emojis or stickers, without words. They
leave the eyes and mouth clean, because those are what create intimacy with someone you know.
