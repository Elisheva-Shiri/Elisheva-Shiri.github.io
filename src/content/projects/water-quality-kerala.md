## The challenge

In India, as in many developing countries, polluted drinking water is a serious problem. Most
water-quality projects rely on lab tests done by experts. Our goal was to let **women monitor the
water in their own households**, with simple tools, to prevent the illnesses that polluted water
causes and to improve quality of life.

## The process

1. **The pollutants.** We studied the main pollutants found in **Kerala**, where the project
   began: pH, E. coli, total dissolved solids, iron, coliform bacteria, turbidity, nitrate and
   more. For each, we looked at how it is tested, the acceptable range, and its short- and
   long-term effects.
2. **Similar projects.** We reviewed projects done in the field and by untrained teams.
3. **Testing tools.** We compared home testing tools, focusing on cheap and simple "dip" tests:
   paper strips or tablets that change colour and are read against a colour chart. We scored
   each by ease of use, the health impact of the pollutant, accuracy, price per test and
   environmental impact.

## Challenges

None of us came from a related background, so the first third of the semester was spent learning
on our own. We also faced internet problems, and many companies had reduced their activity during
the pandemic, so it was hard to get quotes and details about some devices.

## What comes next

We laid the foundation for the project:

- **A testing kit**, assembled from the tests we found.
- **A database** of results, the main goal. The questionnaire needs to be short enough for daily
  use.
- **An app** that collects data offline and sends it when there is a connection, or a paper
  booklet collected monthly.
- In the end, a way to **purify the water**, not only to measure it.

The project was part of a course on **social entrepreneurship and sustainable development**,
supervised by Dr. Vered Blass and Dr. Ram Fishman. I made it with Alexandra Kolpakov
(Economics, TAU) and Yarden Benju (Political Science and Psychology, TAU).
