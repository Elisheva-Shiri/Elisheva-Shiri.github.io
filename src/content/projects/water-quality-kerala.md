## The challenge

In India, as in many developing countries, polluted drinking water is an acute problem. Many
homes store water in containers, so its quality depends not only on the source but also on how
long it sat and how clean the container is. It has to be checked often. Yet a biological test
must be done within six hours of taking the sample, and almost every monitoring project relies
on laboratories and experts that a village household cannot reach or pay for.

Our goal was to let **women monitor the water in their own households**, with simple tools, to
prevent the illnesses that polluted water causes. The project answers **Sustainable Development
Goal 6**, clean water and sanitation, and its target of strengthening local communities in
managing their own water.

## Where it sits

The project is part of a **water monitoring and cleansing venture** of the **Nitzan Lab at Tel
Aviv University**, which studies sustainable development in Africa and Asia, together with
**Amrita University** in Kerala, the venture's link to the community. A team from the lab had already spent several months there: they ran a
questionnaire and collected water samples for laboratory tests. They found many different
pollutants in the drinking water, which is what raised the need for everyday monitoring at
home. Our work was the next step.

## Three ways it should change things

- **Awareness.** Many people live with the effects of polluted water without linking their
  illnesses to it. Seeing a result for your own water is what makes the link.
- **Community.** Several households in one community testing together keep each other going,
  and their results add up to a picture of the whole place.
- **Women's standing.** Women and girls traditionally carry the water. Making them the ones who
  test it, and who protect the household's health, gives them a role the community depends on.

## What we did

1. **The pollutants.** We collected studies of water pollution in Kerala into one table: what was
   measured, where, how it was tested, the permitted level, how often it was exceeded, and
   whether the harm is immediate or long-term. Ten pollutants kept returning: **pH, E. coli,
   coliform bacteria, total dissolved solids, iron, turbidity, nitrate, alkalinity, residual
   chlorine and fluoride**. Those that harm at once, like E. coli, come before those that harm
   over years, like iron or fluoride.
2. **Similar projects.** Most studies we found measure in a laboratory, with expensive
   instruments. I searched for the few that test in the field, cheaply. The most common method is
   the **hydrogen sulphide paper-strip test**, which reveals bacterial pollution without a lab. In
   a study of 622 drinking-water samples in Chile, it predicted bacterial pollution as well as
   the laboratory method.
3. **Does a home test change behaviour?** Yes, according to the studies we reviewed. In 60
   villages in Uttar Pradesh, households that received their own test kits started purifying
   their water 11% more than the other groups. In Ghana, half of the adults and 79% of the
   schoolchildren who were invited chose to join the testing workshops.
4. **The testing tools.** We compared **33 home testing tools**: pocket meters you dip and read,
   paper strips that change colour within seconds, kits where you add a reagent and compare the
   colour, and bacteria tests that take 24 to 48 hours. Each was scored for ease of use (from
   "just dip" to "wait and keep the sample sterile"), the harm of the pollutant it detects,
   accuracy, price per test in rupees, and the waste it leaves behind.

To turn many measurements into one answer, we looked at the **Water Quality Index**, a formula
that weighs several parameters into a single number: is this water fit to drink or not.

## Challenges

None of us came from water engineering, so the first third of the semester went into learning
the field on our own. Testing water in field conditions turned out to be the core difficulty:
keeping the equipment and the sample clean is hard even for a trained team. We worked over video
calls throughout, during the pandemic, when many companies had reduced their activity and it was
hard to get prices and details for some devices.

## What comes next

Our work is a foundation for the teams that continue the project:

- **A testing kit** chosen from the tools we compared: cheap, reusable where possible, easy to
  use and accurate enough to tell "above the norm" from "dangerous now".
- **Two questionnaires.** A longer one every few months, or after a major weather event, about
  habits and the general situation; and a short one, daily or weekly, about the water itself:
  its source, colour, taste and smell.
- **An app that works offline**, or a paper booklet collected monthly, because a steady internet
  connection cannot be assumed. From the answers and the measurement it tells the woman whether
  the water is fit to drink. Bacteria mean "no" at once; metals are recorded over time, to warn
  about long-term harm.
- **Advice on treatment** within the household's means: charcoal, chlorine, iodine or boiling.
- **A shared database**, so that researchers and, later, the authorities can see a pollutant that
  stays in one community and treat it at the source.

## My part

My role was **analytics research**. In the team, Alexandra and I gathered the studies into the
pollutant table, and I then led the literature review of projects that tested water in the field.

I came to this research from the practical side of the same problem: two years earlier I had
worked on the [water filtering and distribution system at Sabai Gardens](../sabai-gardens/), a
community garden in Thailand.

The project was the final work of the course **Introduction to Social Entrepreneurship and
Sustainable Development: Tools and Methods** at Tel Aviv University, taught by Dr. Vered Blass and
Dr. Ram Fishman, and guided at the Nitzan Lab by Selda Edris, with advice from Prof. Hadas Mamane.
I made it with Alexandra Kolpakov (Economics, TAU) and Yarden Bengio (Political Science and
Psychology, TAU).
